# Building a Personality Assessment for Career Development

Career platforms often focus on resumes, applications, and technical skills. But students can also benefit from structured reflection on how they approach work, communication, and challenges.

While working on **TieOpp**, I contributed to a personality assessment experience intended to help students better understand themselves as they explored career opportunities.

## Five dimensions, presented in accessible language

The assessment was inspired by the broad five-factor personality framework. To make the results easier to understand in the platform, we used these labels:

| Personality dimension | TieOpp label |
| --- | --- |
| Openness | Mindset |
| Conscientiousness | Self-Management |
| Extraversion | Interactions |
| Agreeableness | Personality |
| Neuroticism | Resilience |

These are product-facing labels rather than exact scientific synonyms. The goal was to present reflection areas in language that students could relate to.

## How it fit into the student journey

The assessment was part of the student profile experience. The application checked whether a student had completed it before certain internship application flows, and could show a prompt when it was still pending.

Students could **retake the assessment after 10 days**, giving them another opportunity to reflect on their responses. That does not mean personality is expected to change dramatically in ten days; retakes should be interpreted cautiously.

## Why this can be useful

Used thoughtfully, an assessment can encourage students to ask practical questions:

- How do I usually approach unfamiliar problems?
- Do I prefer independent work or frequent collaboration?
- What habits help me manage deadlines?
- How do I respond to setbacks or feedback?

These conversations can inform personal development goals, mentoring discussions, and career exploration.

## Important limitations

A personality score should **not** be treated as a verdict about someone's ability, career potential, or suitability for a job. Results depend on question design, interpretation, language, context, and the honesty and understanding of the respondent.

Assessments also need careful consideration of privacy, accessibility, and fairness. They should support students rather than unnecessarily restrict their opportunities.

## What I took away

Building a useful career feature is not only about calculating a score. It is about **explaining results responsibly**, integrating them into an understandable user journey, and helping people turn reflection into practical next steps.

For me, the most important design principle was to make the assessment a tool for self-awareness—not a label that defines a student.
